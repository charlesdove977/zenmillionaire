## Pay Yourself First (Start Thy Purse to Fattening)
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.14, p.27]

The foundational law: before any bill, tradesman, or desire is paid, a fixed share of every coin earned must be set aside and kept. Arkad frames this as the difference between working for others your whole life versus building an estate. The mechanism is deliberately simple — take one-tenth off the top, every time, without exception, before spending on anything else.

> "A part of all you earn is yours to keep... Pay yourself first." (p.14)
> "For every ten coins thou placest within thy purse take out for use but nine. Thy purse will start to fatten at once." (p.27)

**Apply:** On every payday, move at least 10% to savings/investment first, before any other expense is paid.

## Control Thy Expenditures (Budget Thy Budget)
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.29-31]

Expenses expand to consume income unless deliberately restrained — desires always outnumber the gold available to satisfy them. A budget isn't a cage; it's a light in a dark cave that reveals which "necessary" expenses are actually just unexamined habits, so the fattening tenth is never touched.

> "That what each of us calls our 'necessary expenses' will always grow to equal our incomes unless we protest to the contrary." (p.29)
> "Budget thy expenses that thou mayest have coins to pay for thy necessities... without spending more than nine-tenths of thy earnings." (p.31)

**Apply:** Write down every category of spending and cap total spend at 90% of income, no matter how income grows.

## Make Thy Gold Multiply (Put Your Money to Work)
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.31-33]

Saved gold sitting idle is only the starting seed; the real goal is building an income stream that flows in continuously, whether or not you personally show up to work. Every coin lent or invested should earn, and its earnings should be reinvested so they too earn — turning capital into an army of "golden slaves" that labor on your behalf.

> "A man's wealth is not in the coins he carries in his purse; it is the income he buildeth, the golden stream that continually floweth into his purse." (p.32)
> "This, then, is the third cure for a lean purse: to put each coin to labouring that it may reproduce its kind... and help bring thee income." (p.33)

**Apply:** Reinvest earnings/interest instead of spending them, so returns compound on returns.

## Guard Thy Treasures from Loss
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.33-35]

Protecting principal matters more than chasing outsized returns; the first duty of an investment is that it can be safely reclaimed. Arkad's own first investment (entrusting savings to a brickmaker to buy "jewels" that turned out to be worthless glass) is the cautionary example — never invest in what you or trusted, experienced advisors don't understand.

> "Misfortune loves a shining mark... The first sound principle of investment is security for thy principal." (p.33-34)
> "Consult with wise men. Secure the advice of those experienced in the profitable handling of gold. Let their wisdom protect thy treasure from unsafe investments." (p.35)

**Apply:** Before investing, verify you can get the principal back and get it reviewed by someone with real experience in that specific asset.

## Make of Thy Dwelling a Profitable Investment
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.35-36]

Money paid to a landlord builds no equity and reduces the funds available for other investment. Owning your own home lowers your cost of living, frees up more of your nine-tenths for enjoyment and further investment, and builds a real asset over time instead of renting one.

> "Greatly will it reduce his cost of living, making available more of his earnings for pleasures and the gratification of his desires. This, then, is the fifth cure for a lean purse: Own thy own home." (p.36)

**Apply:** Treat housing cost as an investment decision — buying (when the math works) beats paying rent indefinitely.

## Insure a Future Income
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.37-39]

A working life eventually ends, by choice or by circumstance, so income-producing assets (property, interest-bearing loans, what we'd now call retirement accounts/insurance) must be built up in advance of that day. Small, regular payments compounded over decades produce a "worthy competence" — Clason's parable of the sandal-maker's two-silver-a-week deposits growing to 4,000 pieces over 20 years makes the math concrete.

> "This, then, is the sixth cure for a lean purse. Provide in advance for the needs of thy growing age and the protection of thy family." (p.39)

**Apply:** Start a retirement/insurance provision now, sized to what you can sustain for decades, not what feels urgent today.

## Increase Thy Ability to Earn
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.39-42]

Earning capacity itself compounds: skill, effort, and definite desire (not vague wishing) grow income over time, which is what makes the other six cures possible at increasing scale. Arkad's own path — becoming the best tablet-carver through focused effort and study — precedes his wealth-building, not the other way around.

> "Preceding accomplishment must be desire. Thy desires must be strong and definite... For a man to desire five pieces of gold is a tangible desire which he can press to fulfilment." (p.40)
> "The seventh and last remedy for a lean purse is to cultivate thy own powers, to study and become wiser, to become more skilful, to so act as to respect thyself." (p.42)

**Apply:** Set one specific, sized income goal (not "make more money") and invest in the skill that gets you there.

## The Five Laws of Gold
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.65]

A compact, memorable statement of everything the Seven Cures teach about money's behavior: it grows for savers, works for the informed, is protected by caution, and flees from ignorance, impatience, and schemers. Nomasir only rebuilds his fortune after learning these by heart and applying them, having first lost his inheritance by ignoring them.

> "1. Gold cometh gladly... to any man who will put by not less than one-tenth of his earnings... 4. Gold slippeth away from the man who invests it in businesses or purposes with which he is not familiar... 5. Gold flees the man who would force it to impossible earnings or who followeth the alluring advice of tricksters and schemers." (p.65)

**Apply:** Before any financial decision, check it against all five laws — especially "am I familiar with this" and "am I chasing an unrealistic return."

## Good Luck Favors the Man of Action
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.57-58, p.135]

"Luck" is not random; in Babylon's own debate, every case of missed good fortune traced back to hesitation, and every case of real prosperity traced back to someone who acted on an opportunity or simply did their work well and consistently. The companion parable (Sharru Nada, "The Luckiest Man in Babylon") shows the same idea from a different angle: consistent, willing work — not chance — was the thing that rescued him from slavery and built his fortune.

> "Good luck can be enticed by accepting opportunity... Men of action please her best." (p.57-58)
> "Work, thou see, by this, in the time of my greatest distress, didst prove to be my best friend." (p.135)

**Apply:** Treat every reasonable opportunity as a decision to act on immediately, not to weigh indefinitely — procrastination is the actual enemy, not bad luck.

## The Debt Repayment Plan (Dabasir's Clay Tablets)
[Source: The Richest Man in Babylon, p.108-113]

A concrete system for escaping debt without abandoning savings: split every unit of income into 10% kept, 70% for living expenses, and 20% divided fairly among all creditors on a fixed schedule. Dabasir proves the plan by using it to pay off every debt within about a year while still building savings the entire time — discipline and a fair, visible plan restore both solvency and reputation.

> "Therefore one-tenth of all I earn shall be set aside as my own to keep... Therefore, do I here engrave the name of every man to whom I am indebted and the honest amount of my debt." (p.108-109)
> "Yet it is the plan that hath made my success. It hath enabled me to pay all my debts and to jingle both gold and silver in my purse." (p.113)

**Apply:** If in debt, split income 10/70/20 (savings/living/creditor repayment) and pay every creditor pro-rata on a fixed schedule rather than paying whoever complains loudest.
