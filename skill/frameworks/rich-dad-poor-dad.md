## Assets vs. Liabilities
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.44-48]

Kiyosaki's foundational rule: the rich acquire assets, while the poor and middle class acquire liabilities they mistakenly believe are assets. An asset is anything that puts money in your pocket without your labor (rental income, dividends, interest, royalties); a liability takes money out of your pocket every month (mortgage, car loans, credit card debt). Most people never build wealth because they can't tell the two apart, most famously believing their own home is an asset when it is actually a liability sitting on their balance sheet.

> "Rich people acquire assets. The poor and middle class acquire liabilities that they think are assets." (p.44)
> "An asset puts money in my pocket. A liability takes money out of my pocket." (p.46)

**Apply:** Before any purchase, ask "does this put money in my pocket or take it out" and route spare cash only toward the former.

## The Rich Don't Work for Money (Fear & Greed)
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.9, 29-34]

Rich dad's first lesson to a nine-year-old Kiyosaki: the poor and middle class work for money, while the rich have money work for them. Most people stay trapped in the "Rat Race" because two emotions run their financial lives unchecked, fear of not having money drives them to a job, and greed/desire for what money can buy keeps them spending everything they earn, so the cycle of paycheck-to-paycheck never breaks. The fix isn't eliminating fear and greed (impossible) but observing them instead of reacting to them, so you can think instead of just chase the next paycheck.

> "The poor and the middle class work for money. The rich have money work for them." (p.9)
> "People's lives are forever controlled by two emotions: fear and greed." (p.29)

**Apply:** When an urge to chase a raise, bonus, or purchase hits, pause and name the emotion (fear or desire) before acting on it.

## Financial IQ / Financial Literacy
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.42-43, 104]

Money problems are rarely solved by more money, they're solved by financial intelligence. Kiyosaki illustrates this with the story of 1920s titans who died broke, and argues intelligence, not income, produces lasting wealth. He breaks financial IQ into four technical skills that compound: accounting (reading numbers), investing (making money with money), understanding markets (supply and demand), and the law (using tax and corporate structures legally).

> "Intelligence solves problems and produces money. Money without financial intelligence is money soon gone." (p.42)
> Financial intelligence "is made up of these four main technical skills: 1. Accounting... 2. Investing... 3. Understanding markets... 4. The law." (p.104)

**Apply:** Treat money skills (reading a balance sheet, evaluating a deal, knowing basic tax rules) as a lifelong study, not a one-time class.

## Mind Your Own Business
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.72-73, 76]

Your profession is not your business. A banker who doesn't own the bank, an employee earning a great salary, is still minding someone else's business and building someone else's wealth. Kiyosaki's "secret number three of the rich" is to keep your day job for income and stability, but relentlessly build your own asset column on the side (real estate, stocks, businesses that don't need your presence) rather than chasing raises and promotions that only fatten the income column.

> "The rich focus on their asset columns while everyone else focuses on their income statements." (p.71)
> "Financial struggle is often the result of people working all their lives for someone else." (p.73)

**Apply:** Keep the job, but direct a fixed slice of every paycheck into building your own asset column, not your employer's.

## Pay Yourself First
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.142-144]

Rich dad's money habit: pay yourself first, before bills, before taxes, before anyone else, even when cash is tight. Counterintuitively, this creates pressure, since creditors and tax collectors will yell louder than you will if you shortchange them, and that external pressure forces you to get creative, work harder, and find new income rather than quietly staying broke. Paying yourself last (what most people do) removes that pressure and keeps you financially weak.

> "I firmly believe in paying my bills on time. I just pay myself first. Before I pay even the government." (p.143)
> "If I pay myself first, I get financially stronger, mentally and fiscally." (p.144)

**Apply:** Automate an investment/savings transfer the moment income lands, before a single bill is paid.

## The Rich Invent Money
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.97-98, 103]

Financial intelligence is really about options: the more solutions you can generate for a financial problem, the richer you become, since money itself is largely agreement and illusion rather than a fixed, scarce thing. Kiyosaki's own example, turning $2,000 borrowed for 90 days into a $40,000 asset-column note through a distressed-market house flip, shows how a trained mind can "invent" money out of an opportunity most people can't even see.

> "The single most powerful asset we all have is our mind. If it is trained well, it can create enormous wealth seemingly instantaneously." (p.98)
> "Financial intelligence is simply having more options." (p.97)

**Apply:** When short on cash for an opportunity, brainstorm five financing options before assuming the deal is out of reach.

## Work to Learn, Not Just for Money
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.115, 120, 126]

Rich dad pushed Kiyosaki to seek jobs and skills for what they'd teach him, not for the paycheck or benefits, favoring breadth (sales, marketing, accounting, leadership) over narrow specialization. A secure job that teaches nothing new is, in his words, just "Just Over Broke", it caps your income and leaves you with only one transferable skill if that industry ever collapses.

> "Job security meant everything to my educated dad. Learning meant everything to my rich dad." (p.115)
> "Job is an acronym for 'Just Over Broke.'" (p.120)

**Apply:** When choosing between two roles or opportunities, weight the skills you'll gain over the salary on offer.

## Overcoming Fear of Failure
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.129-133]

The primary difference between rich and poor isn't the presence of fear, everyone fears losing money, it's how each manages that fear. Rich dad's "Texan attitude" (win big, lose big, but never play so small you avoid the game) reframes failure as fuel: winners let a loss inspire them to get better, while losers let it convince them to quit and play it permanently safe.

> "The primary difference between a rich person and a poor person is how they manage fear." (p.129)
> "Failure inspires winners. Failure defeats losers." (p.132)

**Apply:** After a financial loss or bad investment, write down what it teaches you before deciding whether to retreat.

## Overcoming Cynicism (Chicken Little)
[Source: Rich Dad Poor Dad, p.134-137]

Doubt and "noise", the inner and outer chorus warning "the sky is falling", keeps most people frozen out of good opportunities, especially during downturns when the best deals appear. Rich dad's distinction: cynics criticize from the sidelines while winners analyze the actual numbers and act, since the worst-looking markets are often the best times to buy.

> "Cynics criticize, and winners analyze." (p.137)
> "It's those doubts and cynicism that keep most people poor and playing it safe." (p.137)

**Apply:** Before dismissing an opportunity as "too risky," run the actual numbers instead of repeating secondhand doubt.
