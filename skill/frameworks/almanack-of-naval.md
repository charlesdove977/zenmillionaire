## Seek Wealth, Not Money or Status
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.31]
Pole: modern-leverage

Naval opens his whole framework by separating three things people conflate: wealth (assets that earn while you sleep), money (how society transfers time and wealth between people, an IOU), and status (your rank in the social hierarchy). Status is zero-sum and political; wealth is positive-sum and can be created ethically. Chasing status games (attacking wealth creators, office politics) burns the years you should spend building assets.

> "Seek wealth, not money or status. Wealth is having assets that earn while you sleep. Money is how we transfer time and wealth. Status is your place in the social hierarchy." (p.31)
> "Wealth is the thing you want... Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep." (p.38)

**Apply:** Before chasing a raise or a title, ask whether the move buys you an asset that earns without your hours, or just a bigger IOU and a better seat at the status table.

## Find and Build Specific Knowledge
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.33]
Pole: modern-leverage

Specific knowledge is what you were already doing as a kid almost effortlessly, something no school teaches because it can't be trained into anyone at scale, only discovered through curiosity, obsession, and apprenticeship. If society can train someone else to do your job, it can replace you and pay you a commodity wage. Naval says it's found at the edge of what you're genuinely curious about, not whatever field is currently "hot."

> "Specific knowledge is knowledge you cannot be trained for. If society can train you, it can train someone else, and replace you." (p.33)
> "No one can compete with you on being you." (p.41)

**Apply:** List the thing you did obsessively before anyone paid you for it; that's the raw material of your specific knowledge, not your resume line items.

## Take on Accountability
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.50]
Pole: modern-leverage

To attract leverage (capital, labor, an audience), someone has to trust you enough to hand it over, and trust is built by putting your name on outcomes, good and bad. Accountability is a double-edged sword: it lets you take full credit when things go well, but you also absorb the humiliation when they don't. Naval argues the actual downside risk in modern society (personal bankruptcy, a failed launch) is smaller than people fear, so most people under-take accountability rather than over-take it.

> "Embrace accountability, and take business risks under your own name. Society will reward you with responsibility, equity, and leverage." (p.50)
> "Intentions don't matter. Actions do. That's why being ethical is hard." (p.47)

**Apply:** Put your name publicly on the next risky bet instead of hiding behind a committee or a boss; that's how leverage gets handed to you.

## Find a Position of Leverage
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.34]
Pole: modern-leverage

Fortunes require leverage, and there are three kinds: labor (people working for you, the oldest and messiest form), capital (money, the dominant form of the last century), and code or media (products with no marginal cost of replication). The first two are "permissioned": someone has to decide to follow you or fund you. Code and media are "permissionless": anyone can write software, publish a book, or record a podcast without asking anyone, which is why the newest fortunes (and the 1,000x programmer) are built this way.

> "Code and media are permissionless leverage. They're the leverage behind the newly rich. You can create software and media that works for you while you sleep." (p.35)
> "Forget rich versus poor, white-collar versus blue. It's now leveraged versus un-leveraged." (p.59)

**Apply:** Before adding headcount or raising capital, ask if the same leverage could come from something you build once in code or media that keeps working after you stop.

## Play Long-Term Games with Long-Term People
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.32]
Pole: modern-leverage

All the compounding returns in life, wealth, relationships, or knowledge, come from staying in the same game with the same people long enough for trust to compound. Naval picks his industry, his partners, and his deals with a decades-long lens: reputational compounding can make a sterling name worth thousands of times more than a talented but flaky operator's. He explicitly screens for high intelligence, energy, and above all integrity, and refuses to partner with cynics because their pessimism becomes self-fulfilling.

> "Pick an industry where you can play long-term games with long-term people... All the returns in life, whether in wealth, relationships, or knowledge, come from compound interest." (p.32)
> "When you find the right thing to do, when you find the right people to work with, invest deeply. Sticking with it for decades is really how you make the big returns." (p.48)

**Apply:** Before signing a deal, ask if this is a person you'd still want to be in business with in ten years; if not, the short-term upside isn't worth it.

## Get Paid for Your Judgment
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.67]
Pole: modern-leverage

Once you have specific knowledge, accountability, and leverage, the real prize is being paid for your judgment rather than your hours. A leveraged worker with slightly better judgment (right 85% of the time instead of 75%) can be worth tens of millions more, because small differences in decision quality get massively amplified by the leverage behind them. Naval's synthesis of this whole wealth-building path is the mnemonic "productize yourself": productize captures leverage and specific knowledge, yourself captures uniqueness and accountability.

> "I want a robot, capital, or computer to do the work, but I want to be paid for my judgment." (p.68)
> "Productize yourself... 'Yourself' has uniqueness. 'Productize' has leverage. 'Yourself' has accountability. 'Productize' has specific knowledge." (p.37)

**Apply:** Structure your work so you're tracked and paid on outcomes and decisions, not on hours logged; that's the exit from the labor trap.

## Happiness Is a Choice (and a Learnable Skill)
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.128]
Pole: mindset-zen

Naval treats happiness the way he treats fitness or nutrition: a trainable skill, not a trait you're handed or a circumstance you stumble into. He rated his own happiness a 2-3/10 for most of his life and moved it to a 9/10 through deliberate practice (reducing desires, meditating, curating who he spends time with), not through winning more games. Because it's learned, he argues, anyone can start improving their baseline immediately.

> "Happiness is a choice you make and a skill you develop." (p.134)
> "You can very slowly but steadily and methodically improve your happiness baseline, just like you can improve your fitness." (p.133)

**Apply:** Stop waiting to feel happy and instead pick one small daily habit (a meditation sit, an unplug from social media) that trains the skill directly.

## Every Desire Is a Chosen Unhappiness
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.137]
Pole: mindset-zen

Naval's sharpest reframe: every desire is a contract you sign with yourself to be unhappy until you get the thing you want. The car, the promotion, the relationship milestone, none of it delivers lasting relief because the mind simply moves on to the next desire (hedonic adaptation). He doesn't say stop wanting things entirely; he says stay aware you're choosing your suffering, and keep the list of active desires to one at a time.

> "Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want." (p.138)
> "I try not to have more than one big desire in my life at any given time, and I also recognize it as the axis of my suffering." (p.138)

**Apply:** Name your current big want out loud, then ask honestly whether you're choosing that unhappiness on purpose or drifting into it by default.

## Peace Is the Goal, Not Joy
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.135]
Pole: mindset-zen

Naval distinguishes joy or bliss (a fleeting peak) from peace (a stable baseline), and says peace is what he's actually after. A happy person isn't someone who feels ecstatic all the time; it's someone who "effortlessly interprets events" so they never lose their innate peace. Most people chase peace by trying to resolve every external problem, but external problems are infinite, so the only real path to peace is internal: giving up the belief that solving the outside world is what will settle the inside.

> "You'll notice when I say happiness, I mean peace... I'll take peace." (p.136)
> "A happy person isn't someone who's happy all the time. It's someone who effortlessly interprets events in such a way that they don't lose their innate peace." (p.136)

**Apply:** Next time you're chasing a fix for an external problem to "finally relax," check whether the real fix is dropping the story you're telling about it instead.

## Happiness Requires Presence
[Source: The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, p.134]
Pole: mindset-zen

Most of the mind's activity is spent replaying the past or rehearsing the future, which is exactly the time you're not experiencing what's actually in front of you. Naval says he doesn't dwell on his past at all, no nostalgia, no regret, because comparing the present to a remembered past is a direct source of unhappiness. The present moment is also literally all that exists: no one has ever gone back in time or successfully predicted the future in a way that mattered.

> "The more present I am, the happier and more content I will be." (p.131)
> "There is actually nothing but this moment... the only thing that exists is this exact point where you are in space at the exact time you happen to be here." (p.202)

**Apply:** When you catch your mind rehashing yesterday or rehearsing tomorrow, name it ("planning" or "regretting") and bring attention back to the one thing happening right now.
