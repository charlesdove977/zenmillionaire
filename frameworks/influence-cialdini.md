## Reciprocation
[Source: Influence, p.13-14]

Humans carry a deep, evolved obligation to repay what others give them — a favor, a gift, a concession — even when the gift was unsolicited. Because the debt is felt before it's examined, whoever gives first controls the exchange: a free sample, an unsolicited favor, or a small kindness creates disproportionate pressure to reciprocate, often with something far more valuable than what was given.

> "The rule says that we should try to repay, in kind, what another person has provided us." (p.13)
> Christmas cards mailed to total strangers came "pouring back" from people who had never heard of the sender — "click, and whirr, they automatically sent one in return." (p.13)

**Apply:** Earn it — give real value first (a genuine sample, insight, or favor) with no strings, and let reciprocity work honestly. Defend against it — when someone hands you an unsolicited "gift," separate whether you like the offer from whether you feel you owe them; you can keep the gift and still say no.

## Rejection-Then-Retreat (Contrast + Reciprocal Concessions)
[Source: Influence, p.9, p.28-29]

A large, likely-to-be-refused request followed by a smaller "real" request reads as a concession — triggering the reciprocity rule to concede back with a yes. It's amplified by the contrast principle: a second, smaller ask looks even smaller placed right after a big one. This is the psychological engine behind "anchor high, then come down to your real price."

> "The technique is a simple one that we can call the rejection-then-retreat technique... I should view your second request as a concession to me and should feel inclined to respond with a concession of my own." (p.28)
> On perceptual contrast: "if the second item is fairly different from the first, we will tend to see it as more different than it actually is." (p.9)

**Apply:** Earn it — anchor with your real best offer's full scope so the final price looks like a genuine concession, not a manufactured one. Defend against it — evaluate every "final offer" against your own walk-in budget, not against the inflated first number you were shown.

## Commitment and Consistency
[Source: Influence, p.43-44, p.57]

Once someone takes a small stand or makes a small commitment (especially in writing, in public, or seen as self-chosen), they feel internal pressure to act consistently with it, even reshaping their self-image and beliefs to justify it after the fact. Small initial commitments (a signature, a survey answer, a free trial signup) are the lever compliance professionals use to grow into large ones later — because the person now sees themselves as "the kind of person who does this."

> "It is, quite simply, our nearly obsessive desire to be (and to appear) consistent with what we have already done." (p.43)
> "You can use small commitments to manipulate a person's self-image... once you've got a man's self-image where you want it, he should comply naturally with a whole range of your requests that are consistent with this view of himself." (p.57)

**Apply:** Earn it — invite a small, genuine first step (a free consult, a tiny paid pilot) that lets a real yes compound honestly into a bigger one. Defend against it — before honoring a past commitment, re-ask "knowing what I know now, would I choose this today?" instead of auto-continuing to stay consistent.

## Social Proof
[Source: Influence, p.87, p.89]

People decide what's correct, especially under uncertainty, by watching what other people like them are already doing — a mental shortcut that works well on average but can be gamed with fabricated or partial evidence of "everyone else is doing it." Canned laughter, salted tip jars, seeded testimonials, and "bestseller" labels all exploit this shortcut directly.

> "I don't know anyone who likes canned laughter... Yet they religiously employ the laugh tracks that their audiences find distasteful." (p.87)
> "We have become so accustomed to taking the humorous reactions of others as evidence of what deserves laughter that we, too, can be made to respond to the sound and not to the substance of the real thing." (p.89)

**Apply:** Earn it — show real customer proof (genuine testimonials, real usage numbers) instead of manufacturing the appearance of a crowd. Defend against it — when a decision feels obvious because "everyone's buying/doing this," pause and ask what evidence you'd need if no one else were watching.

## Liking
[Source: Influence, p.126, p.129]

People say yes far more readily to those they like, and liking is manufactured predictably through similarity, compliments, familiarity, physical attractiveness (the "halo effect," where good looks unconsciously imply talent and honesty), and cooperative shared effort. Compliance professionals engineer likability deliberately rather than earning it, because strangers will grant favors to someone they've simply been made to like.

> "As a rule, we most prefer to say yes to the requests of someone we know and like." (p.126)
> "A halo effect occurs when one positive characteristic of a person dominates the way that person is viewed by others. And the evidence is now clear that physical attractiveness is often such a characteristic." (p.129)

**Apply:** Earn it — build genuine rapport and real similarity with a prospect before you ask for the sale; likability that isn't manufactured compounds into trust. Defend against it — if you find yourself agreeing largely because you like the person asking, separate "I like them" from "this is a good deal" before you sign.

## Authority
[Source: Influence, p.166]

People defer to perceived authority so automatically that the mere symbols of expertise — titles, uniforms, expensive clothes, credentials — can trigger compliance even when no real authority or expertise is present. The Milgram obedience experiments showed ordinary people delivering what they believed were dangerous shocks simply because a man in a lab coat told them to continue.

> "The appearance of authority was enough... when in a click, whirr mode, we are often as vulnerable to the symbols of authority as to the substance." (p.166)
> "Con artists... drape themselves with the titles, clothes, and trappings of authority... they understand that when they are so equipped, their chances for compliance are greatly increased." (p.166)

**Apply:** Earn it — lead with verifiable proof of expertise (real results, credentials, case studies) rather than costume-deep authority signals. Defend against it — ask whether a claim would still convince you if the title, uniform, or credential were stripped away and only the evidence remained.

## Scarcity
[Source: Influence, p.179, p.184]

Opportunities seem more valuable the moment they appear limited in availability, deadline, or exclusivity — because losing a freedom to choose is felt more sharply than gaining an equivalent benefit, a bias amplified by psychological reactance (we want what we're told we can't have). Limited-quantity and limited-time framing works even when the scarcity is manufactured, which is why it's the most commonly abused of all six weapons.

> "Since that encounter with the scarcity principle — that opportunities seem more valuable to us when their availability is limited — I have begun to notice its influence over a whole range of my actions." (p.179)
> "As opportunities become less available, we lose freedoms; and we hate to lose the freedoms we already have." (p.184)

**Apply:** Earn it — only advertise scarcity that's real (an actual cart-close date, an actual cohort cap); real limits create urgency without the trust cost of fake countdowns. Defend against it — when urgency spikes your desire for something, ask if you'd still want it at the same price with no deadline attached.
